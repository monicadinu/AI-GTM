Build demo scripts and prep materials that start with the outcome, lead with the magic, and go deep only when earned.

## The Outcome-First Demo Framework

This framework synthesizes the best of five proven methodologies with a practitioner overlay:

**Peter Cohan (Great Demo!)** -- "Do the Last Thing First." Show the end deliverable immediately. Use the Inverted Pyramid so the most important content comes first. Apply the "Fewest Number of Clicks" principle. Peel Back the Layers progressively.

**Rob Falcone (Just F*ing Demo!)** -- Show "just enough" of the product aligned with their immediate problems. Demos are backdrops for conversations about the audience's goals. The 80% in the middle is where you win.

**Steli Efti (Product Demos That Sell)** -- Discovery before demo. Qualify attendees. Keep it focused. Follow up relentlessly.

**April Dunford (Sales Pitch)** -- Differentiated value is the heart of the pitch. "Why pick us over the alternatives?" Weave the demo into the narrative, not as a standalone feature tour.

**Gong.io research (3M+ demos analyzed)** -- Inverted pyramid validated. Start with the use case most discussed in discovery. Speaker switches every ~76 seconds in winning demos. Top producers' buyers ask 28% more questions. Before/after stories outperform big-name logo drops.

**Monica Dinu's practitioner overlay** -- Always start with the OUTCOME for the specific person you're addressing. State what makes you different versus the incumbent or existing technology. Lead with your magic. Go into features and details only when prompted or earned.

## Framework Structure

Every demo follows six phases. Not every phase has equal weight -- the first three phases do the heavy lifting. Phase 4 is earned, not assumed. Phase 5 is non-negotiable. Phase 6 is how this skill gets better over time.

### Phase 0: Pre-Work (before any scripting begins)

**Tier 1 -- Must have before proceeding.** Do not start scripting without these. If the user hasn't provided them, ask.

1. **Product**: What is the product and what does it do? (If a company-research-skill output, positioning-dunford output, or website URL exists, use that.)
2. **Capabilities & features**: What is the product's full list of capabilities and features? Look for existing documentation first -- product pages, feature lists, positioning-dunford output, sales-onepager output, or a capabilities matrix. If none exists, ask the user to provide or confirm the list. This is the inventory you select from in Phases 3 and 4 -- you never demo a capability that isn't on this list, and you never invent features the product doesn't have.
3. **Audience**: Who is watching this demo? Role, seniority, buying stage, technical sophistication.
4. **Industry / ICP type**: Which vertical or segment is this demo for? (e.g., meat manufacturing, dairy manufacturing, soft drinks, logistics, real estate development.) Always ask -- never assume. The industry shapes the data shown, the language used, the use cases selected, and the pain points emphasized.
5. **Primary pain / desired outcome**: What is the #1 thing this audience wants to achieve? What does success look like for THEM (not for the product)?
6. **Mode**: Pre-recorded demo or live demo prep? (If unclear, ask.)
7. **Alternatives**: What are they comparing against? Prompt explicitly for three categories: (a) competitors in this specific deal, (b) spreadsheets / manual processes / Excel files, and (c) the status quo (doing nothing, staying with the current vendor or workflow). Do not infer silently -- ask.
8. **Differentiator / The Magic**: What makes this product uniquely positioned to deliver the outcome versus each alternative above? The mechanism, architecture, or approach that no alternative can replicate. If the user doesn't have clear differentiators ready, do not skip this -- facilitate. Walk them through each alternative category and ask: "What happens when a [persona] tries to solve this with [alternative]? Where does it break? What can your product do there that [alternative] can't?" Help them tease out what's special and how to de-position each alternative. The magic must be articulated before scripting begins.

**Tier 2 -- Confirm during or infer.** These improve the demo but don't block it. Use sensible defaults, research, or derive from Tier 1 inputs. Confirm with the user before finalizing the demo brief, not before starting work.

9. **Length**: Target duration. Pre-recorded defaults to 3-5 minutes. Live defaults to 20-30 minutes (including discovery and Q&A time). (Use defaults unless the user specifies otherwise.)
10. **Demo environment**: Is there a live product instance to show? Sandbox? Screenshots only? This shapes what's possible. (Assume live product instance unless told otherwise.)

**The Demo's Opinion.** After gathering Tier 1 inputs, distill the demo's opinion -- the single thesis that connects the outcome, the industry context, and the magic into one point of view. This is not a tagline or a value prop. It is the opinionated frame that shapes every word and every screen in the demo. For an F&B manufacturer, the opinion might be "cash and margin" -- poor demand forecasting either ties up too much cash in expiring inventory or leaves you short on orders, and either road leaks your margin. For a real estate developer, the opinion might be "cost certainty kills surprises" -- the gap between estimated and actual project costs is where deals go wrong. Name the opinion explicitly in the demo brief. If the demo doesn't have a point of view, it's a product tour.

### Phase 1: THE OUTCOME (opens the demo)

This is Cohan's "Do the Last Thing First" fused with Monica Dinu's "start with the outcome."

Open with the end result. Show the audience what their world looks like AFTER they use this product. The deliverable. The dashboard. The report. The workflow completed. The alert that fired. The insight that surfaced.

Do NOT open with:
- A company overview or "about us" slide
- A feature list
- A market landscape
- A history of the company
- A login screen

The audience should see the payoff in the first 30-60 seconds and think: "I want that."

**Pre-recorded**: This is the opening shot. The voiceover says something like: "This is what [outcome] looks like in [Product]. [Persona] gets [specific result] in [timeframe]. Here's how."

**Live**: This is the first thing you show on screen after a brief personal connection and agenda set. "Before I walk you through anything, let me show you where we're headed. This is what [outcome] looks like for a [their role] at a company like yours."

### Phase 2: THE MAGIC (immediately after the outcome)

This is Monica Dinu's "state what makes you different" + Dunford's "differentiated value."

Now that they've seen the outcome, explain WHY this product can deliver it when alternatives can't. This is the mechanism, the architecture, the approach -- not a feature list, but the underlying reason the product works differently.

Frame it against the incumbent or status quo:
- "Traditional tools do X. We do Y because of Z."
- "Most [category] products require [painful thing]. [Product] eliminates that because [mechanism]."
- "The reason you're seeing that result in [timeframe] instead of [longer timeframe] is [differentiator]."

Keep this to 60-90 seconds. It's a bridge, not a lecture. The audience should understand WHY before you show HOW.

### Phase 3: THE USE CASES (the core of the demo)

Walk through 2-3 primary use cases using the Inverted Pyramid -- most important use case first.

Each use case follows a mini-arc:
1. **Situation**: "You're a [role] and you need to [task]. Today you [painful current method]."
2. **Action**: Show the product doing the thing. Fewest number of clicks. No detours.
3. **Result**: Show the output, the saved time, the eliminated error, the new capability.

Rules for use cases:
- Lead with the use case that maps to the audience's #1 pain (from Phase 0 discovery or brief).
- Each use case should be self-contained enough that if the demo ended here, the audience would still get value.
- Use the industry / ICP type from Phase 0 to select sector-specific data, terminology, and KPIs for each use case. A dairy manufacturing CFO should see yield loss percentages and batch cost variance, not generic "Acme Corp" numbers. A logistics director should see route efficiency and carrier spend, not manufacturing language. If the demo data doesn't speak the audience's industry language, the demo fails before the product gets a chance.
- In live demos, pause after each use case to check in: "Is this the kind of thing you're dealing with?" This is where Gong's speaker-switch data matters -- winning demos have frequent back-and-forth.

### Phase 4: THE DEPTH (earned, not assumed)

Go deeper into features, technical details, integrations, admin settings, and edge cases ONLY when:
- The audience explicitly asks
- The buying stage demands it (technical evaluation, security review, IT due diligence)
- A specific objection needs to be addressed with evidence

In pre-recorded demos, this often becomes a separate "deep dive" video linked from the main demo, or an optional chapter at the end with a clear "skip ahead" option.

In live demos, this is the flexible portion. Have the screens ready, but don't show them unless prompted. Prepare 3-5 "back pocket" deep dives based on common questions for this audience type.

### Phase 5: THE CLOSE (non-negotiable)

Every demo ends with three things:

1. **Outcome recap**: Return to the Phase 1 outcome. "So what you've seen is [outcome] -- [persona] gets [result] in [timeframe]." Mirror the opening.
2. **Social proof**: One before/after story from a similar customer. Not a logo wall -- a specific, concrete result. "A [similar company] went from [before state] to [after state] in [timeframe]." Gong data shows before/after stories outperform big-name drops.
3. **Clear next step**: One specific CTA. Not "let us know if you have questions." For pre-recorded: "Book a live walkthrough at [link]" or "Start your free trial." For live: propose the next meeting with specific attendees and agenda.

### Phase 6: SKILL LEARNING LOOP (after every run)

After delivering the demo output and receiving the user's feedback, observe what worked, what was changed, and what was rejected. Use these observations to propose concrete improvements to this skill file.

**What to capture:**
- Feedback on structure: Did the user reorder phases, skip a phase, or add a section the skill doesn't account for?
- Feedback on content: Did the user rewrite voiceover lines, change terminology, reject a use case framing, or correct industry-specific language?
- Feedback on process: Did the user say Phase 0 asked for too much or too little? Did they want a different output format? Did they need an artifact this skill doesn't produce?
- Prospect reactions (for live demos): If the user reports back after delivering the demo, capture the tone of the prospect, their doubts, the questions they asked, where they pushed back, what landed well, and their overall sentiment. This is a different data stream from creator-side feedback -- it tells you whether the demo's opinion resonated with the actual audience, not just whether the script was well-structured.
- Patterns across runs: If the same correction appears in two or more runs, it's a skill gap, not a one-off preference. If prospect reactions across multiple runs show the same doubts or questions surfacing, that signals a gap in the demo's narrative -- consider adding a pre-emptive answer or adjusting Phase 2 (magic) framing.

**What to do with it:**
1. At the end of each run, after the user has reviewed the output and given feedback, surface a brief "Skill observations" note listing 1-3 specific things this run revealed.
2. For each observation, propose the exact edit to SKILL.md -- the section, the current text, and the replacement text.
3. Do NOT self-edit. Present the proposed changes to the user and wait for sign-off before applying.
4. If the user approves, apply the edit immediately using str_replace.

**What NOT to do:**
- Do not silently change the skill. Every edit requires explicit user approval.
- Do not log vague observations like "user seemed to prefer shorter demos." Be specific: "User cut Phase 2 from 90 seconds to 30 seconds for the third consecutive run -- consider reducing the default guidance from 60-90 seconds to 30-60 seconds."
- Do not propose changes that contradict the core framework principles (outcome first, inverted pyramid, earned depth). If the user's feedback conflicts with a core principle, flag the tension rather than auto-accepting.

## Mode-Specific Instructions

### Pre-Recorded Demo

#### Script Format

The script is a two-column document: voiceover text on the left, screen directions on the right. Each row is one "beat" -- a single idea paired with a single screen state. A beat should never try to say two things at once or show two screens at once.

```
| Voiceover | Screen Direction |
|-----------|-----------------|
| "This is what [outcome] looks like for a [role] at a [industry] company." | Show the end-state dashboard / report / deliverable. Full screen, no clicks yet. Hold 3-4 seconds before VO starts to let the visual land. |
```

Each phase (Outcome, Magic, Use Cases, Depth) starts on a new section header in the script with a timing target.

#### Timing Guidance

Total target: 3-5 minutes unless the user specifies otherwise.

| Phase | Time allocation | Notes |
|-------|----------------|-------|
| Phase 1: Outcome | 30-45 seconds | One screen. One statement. Let it breathe. |
| Phase 2: Magic | 45-60 seconds | Maximum two screens (before/after or side-by-side comparison). This is a bridge, not a lecture. |
| Phase 3: Use Cases | 90-150 seconds | 2-3 use cases at 45-60 seconds each. Most important first. Each use case is situation-action-result. |
| Phase 4: Depth | 0-30 seconds | Only include if a specific deep-dive was requested. Otherwise, omit entirely or add as a separate linked video. |
| Phase 5: Close | 20-30 seconds | Outcome recap, one social proof beat, one CTA. |

If the script runs over 5 minutes, cut use cases (not outcome or magic). Two strong use cases beat three rushed ones.

#### Screen Direction Conventions

Write screen directions as if briefing a screen recorder, not a cinematographer. Be specific about what's visible, what's clicked, and what's highlighted.

- **Show**: A static screen. "Show the demand planning dashboard with the 30-day forecast view active."
- **Click**: A specific interaction. "Click the 'Generate Forecast' button. Pause 1 second on the loading state, then show the result."
- **Highlight**: Call attention to a specific area. "Highlight the MAPE metric in the top-right card (zoom or annotation)."
- **Transition**: Moving between screens. "Transition to the inventory view via the left nav. No animation -- hard cut."
- **Hold**: Let a screen sit. "Hold on this view for 3 seconds before voiceover begins." Use holds after outcome reveals and after use case results.
- **Split/Compare**: Side-by-side. "Split screen: left shows the ERP forecast (static spreadsheet), right shows [Product] forecast (live, updating)."

Do not write vague directions like "show the product" or "navigate around." Every direction should be reproducible by someone who has never seen the product, given access to the demo environment.

#### Voiceover Writing Rules

1. **Conversational, not scripted.** Write the way a confident practitioner would explain it over coffee. No marketing voice. No "leverage" or "empower" or "seamlessly." If it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it.

2. **Second person.** Talk to the viewer: "You're looking at..." / "What this means for you..." / "Instead of spending your Friday afternoon..." Not "Users can..." or "The platform enables..."

3. **Industry language.** Use the terminology from Phase 0 industry input. SKUs not products (F&B). IRR not ROI (real estate). Batch yield not output (manufacturing). If you don't know the right term, flag it for the user rather than defaulting to generic language.

4. **Short sentences.** Voiceover needs breathing room. 12-18 words per sentence maximum. Read it aloud -- if you can't say it in one breath, split it.

5. **One idea per beat.** Each voiceover segment makes one point. "This dashboard shows you your cash tied up in inventory, broken down by SKU and shelf life" -- that's one idea. Don't follow it immediately with "and also you can see the production schedule and the supplier lead times" -- that's a second beat.

6. **Signpost transitions.** Between phases, use a brief verbal bridge: "So that's what the result looks like. Now let me show you why [Product] can deliver this when your ERP can't." / "Now let's see this in action with a real use case."

7. **No feature narration.** Never say "Now I'm going to click on Settings." The screen shows what you're clicking. The voiceover explains WHY it matters: "To get this level of accuracy, you need the right inputs. Here's how you connect your CRM data in under two minutes."

8. **The opinion runs through.** The demo's opinion (from Phase 0) should be the undercurrent of the voiceover. In an F&B demo where the opinion is "cash and margin," the voiceover returns to cash impact at every transition, not just in the opening.

### Live Demo Prep

#### Talk Track Format

The talk track is NOT a verbatim script. It's phase-by-phase bullet-level guidance that a seller can internalize and deliver naturally. Each phase gets:

- **Intent**: One sentence on what this phase accomplishes.
- **Key points**: 2-4 bullets of what to say, phrased as the core idea (not exact words). Example: "Connect poor forecasting to cash trapped in inventory -- either overstocked and expiring, or understocked and losing orders. Both leak margin." The seller decides how to say it.
- **Screen**: Which screen to be on and what to show. Specific enough to follow without rehearsal.
- **Checkpoint**: Where to pause and engage (see Discovery Checkpoints below).
- **If/then branches**: "If they ask about [X], go to [screen]. If they push back on [Y], use [objection handle #N]."

Do not write the talk track as prose paragraphs. Sellers scan, they don't read. Bullets, short phrases, and clear labels.

#### Discovery Checkpoint Placement

Place 3-5 discovery checkpoints across the demo. A checkpoint is a planned pause where the seller stops showing and starts listening. Winning demos (per Gong.io data) have frequent speaker switches -- roughly every 76 seconds.

**Where to place them:**

1. **After Phase 1 (Outcome)**: "Is this the kind of result you're looking for? What does [outcome] look like in your world today?" -- This validates you're solving the right problem before investing 15 minutes in use cases.

2. **After Phase 2 (Magic)**: "Does that match how you're doing it today? What's your current process for [the thing the magic replaces]?" -- This surfaces the real incumbent process, which may differ from what you assumed.

3. **After each use case in Phase 3**: "Is this the kind of thing you're dealing with?" / "How are you handling this today?" / "Who on your team would be using this part?" -- This checks resonance and identifies whether you're talking to the right stakeholder for this use case.

4. **Before Phase 4 (Depth)**: "What questions do you have about what you've seen so far?" -- This earns the right to go deep rather than assuming. If they have no questions, skip Phase 4 entirely and go to the close.

5. **Objection-triggered**: Any time the prospect raises a doubt, concern, or "what about..." -- that's an unplanned checkpoint. Pause. Listen fully. Respond. Then ask if the response addressed their concern before moving on.

**Checkpoint discipline:** Do not ask a checkpoint question and then immediately keep talking. Pause. Wait for the answer. If the answer reveals a pain or priority you hadn't anticipated, acknowledge it and adjust the remaining demo flow on the fly.

#### Objection Handle Structure

Prepare 3-5 likely objections based on the audience role, industry, buying stage, and alternatives from Phase 0. Each handle follows a three-part structure:

1. **Acknowledge**: Mirror the concern back without defensiveness. "So you're asking whether [restate their concern]..."
2. **Reframe**: Connect the objection to the demo's opinion or to a broader business reality. "The reason that matters is [tie to opinion]. Here's what we see with companies like yours..."
3. **Evidence**: Show proof -- a screen, a metric, a customer example. Not a claim. "Let me show you how [similar company] handled exactly this." / "Here's what that looks like in the product."

Do not prepare handles as scripted rebuttals. Prepare them as thinking frameworks the seller can adapt in the moment. The acknowledge-reframe-evidence structure keeps the conversation collaborative rather than adversarial.

**Common objection categories to prepare for:**

- "We already have something that does this" (incumbent/status quo defense)
- "How is this different from [competitor]?" (competitive differentiation)
- "This looks great but we don't have the resources to implement" (adoption/change management)
- "What about [edge case / exception / unusual workflow]?" (depth request -- route to Phase 4)
- "Our data is [messy / siloed / sensitive]" (integration/security concern)

#### Fallback Planning

Things break. Demos crash. Environments go down. Internet drops. The fallback plan ensures the demo's opinion still gets delivered even when the product can't be shown live.

**Prepare three layers:**

1. **Screenshot deck**: 5-8 annotated screenshots that walk through the same Outcome-Magic-Use Cases-Close arc. Not a marketing deck -- actual product screenshots with callouts. This is the "demo goes down entirely" fallback. Have it open in a separate tab before the call starts.

2. **Narrative pivot**: A pre-rehearsed version of the demo that can be delivered purely as a story, with no screens at all. "Let me walk you through what a [persona] at a company like yours experiences in a typical week..." This works when screen-sharing fails completely. It leans on the demo's opinion and the before/after structure.

3. **Partial recovery**: If only one part of the product is down, know which use cases can be shown with the remaining functionality and which should be skipped. Don't try to demo a broken feature and narrate around it -- that signals the product is fragile. Skip it, note it, and offer a follow-up.

**Before every live demo:**

- Open the demo environment 10 minutes early and verify the data is loaded and the key screens work.
- Have the screenshot deck open in a background tab.
- Know which use case you'd cut first if time runs short (always cut the least important use case, never cut the outcome or close).
- Test screen sharing before the prospect joins.

#### Attendee Role Mapping

When multiple stakeholders attend a live demo, map each use case and checkpoint to the person it's most relevant to. Address them by name: "Maria, this is the part I think matters most for your team -- the production scheduling view."

If you know the attendee list from Phase 0:
- Assign each use case a primary audience member.
- Plan at least one checkpoint directed at each attendee by name.
- If the executive buyer is present, the Outcome (Phase 1) and Close (Phase 5) speak directly to them. The use cases speak to the champion/user.
- If IT/security is present, Phase 4 (Depth) is where they engage. Don't front-load technical details for the whole room -- address IT specifically when their section comes.

## Output Format

### For pre-recorded demos, produce:

1. **Demo brief** (one page): audience, outcome, differentiator, use cases selected, target length
2. **Full script**: Section-by-section with voiceover text, screen directions, timing per section, transition notes
3. **Shot list** (optional, if requested): Screen-by-screen breakdown of what to capture

### For live demo prep, produce:

1. **Demo brief** (one page): audience, outcome, differentiator, use cases selected, expected duration, attendee roles
2. **Talk track**: Phase-by-phase talking points (not a verbatim script -- bullet-level guidance)
3. **Click path**: The exact screens and sequence to follow, with "if they ask about X, go to Y" branches
4. **Discovery checkpoints**: 3-5 moments to pause, ask a question, and listen
5. **Objection handles**: 3-5 likely objections with suggested responses
6. **Fallback plan**: What to do if the demo breaks (backup slides, narrative pivots)

## Key Principles (apply to both modes)

1. **Outcome before mechanism, mechanism before features.** Always.
2. **Inverted pyramid within every section.** Most important thing first, within every phase and every use case.
3. **Fewest clicks possible.** Every click that doesn't advance the story is a click that loses attention.
4. **Realistic data.** Generic data signals a generic product. Match the audience's world.
5. **The demo is not the product tour.** A product tour shows everything. A demo shows just enough to create a buying decision.
6. **Earn the right to go deep.** Features and technical details are Phase 4 -- earned through engagement, not dumped upfront.
7. **Speaker switches matter.** In live demos, plan for interaction every 60-90 seconds. In pre-recorded demos, vary the pacing and visual rhythm to maintain attention.
8. **Before/after over logos.** One concrete customer transformation story beats twenty logos on a slide.
9. **One CTA, not a menu.** End with one clear next step. Decision fatigue kills momentum.
10. **The best demo is a conversation.** Even pre-recorded demos should feel like someone talking to you, not at you.

## Integration with Other Skills

This skill works well in sequence with:
- **positioning-dunford**: Run positioning first to define competitive alternatives, differentiated value, and best-fit customer. Feed the output directly into Phase 0.
- **narrative-raskin**: If the demo needs a strategic narrative wrapper (especially for executive or investor audiences), layer the Raskin narrative structure around the demo.
- **sales-b2b**: For live demos embedded in a sales cycle, the Farrokh/Nasralla frameworks handle pre-call prep, discovery, and deal advancement around the demo itself.
- **behavioral-psychology-marketing**: Apply Cialdini/Harhut/Shotton principles to the voiceover copy and talk track for maximum persuasive impact.
- **company-research-skill**: Research the prospect's company before building the demo to customize data, language, and use case selection.
